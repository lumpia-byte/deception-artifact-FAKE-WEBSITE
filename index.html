Dissection key

  1. Borrowed authority: a "Department" name, seal and navy-and-gold styling mimic government without any legal power.
  2. Fake endorsement: real U.S. agencies like the FDA do not endorse or approve individual supplements before sale. A "Gold Seal" from an invented office means nothing.
  3. Unverifiable details: review IDs, dates, percentages and counts have no source, methodology or link to records.
  4. Embellishment: "fully complies," "breakthrough" and "9 out of 10" are absolute or exaggerated claims.
  5. Circular logic: the product's own marketer could be the one hosting the "independent" endorsement. Check who owns the site and who benefits.
  6. Missing: a real address, named reviewers, a .gov domain, a regulation cited, or a way to confirm the claim elsewhere.
  7. Verify instead: consult sources such as the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, the FDA's consumer pages, and independent testing programs.
